# Think Like a Manager: Risk, Ethics and the Exam — Avatar Tutor Script

**Lesson ID:** cissp-mindset
**Track:** CISSP Domains
**Estimated runtime:** ~6 minutes (~775 words)

## Spoken script

**[HOOK — 0:00]**
Here is the uncomfortable truth about the CISSP: most people who fail it are
excellent engineers. They know the technology cold. They lose because they
answer like technicians, when the exam rewards the thinking of a risk advisor
to the business. This lesson rewires how you think before you touch any of the
eight domains.

**[WHY IT MATTERS — ~0:25]**
Every domain in this course is secretly a management scenario wearing a
technical costume. When a question asks what to do first, the CISSP is testing
whether you align security with business goals — not whether you know the
fanciest tool. Nail this mindset and the trickiest scenario questions become
predictable.

**[SECTION — The priorities behind every answer — ~0:50]**
Three priorities sit behind every CISSP answer. The first: human safety comes
first, always. Life safety beats data, systems and money in every scenario —
which is why emergency exits fail open. You can restore data from a backup; you
cannot restore a person. If two answers both look right, pick the one that
protects people.

Second: senior management is ultimately accountable for security.
Responsibility can be delegated to security teams — accountability cannot.
Think of a ship's captain: the crew can steer, but the captain answers for the
voyage. CISSP answers favour the viewpoint of a risk advisor to the business,
never the technician. Ask yourself: which option would a calm advisor recommend
to the board?

Third: before choosing any control, understand the risk and the business
requirements. Assess, then treat. Technology is rarely the first answer. And in
most scenarios, the best first step is to gather facts or consult policy and
stakeholders — not to take drastic unilateral action. A doctor diagnoses before
prescribing; a risk advisor assesses before spending.

**[SECTION — Due care, ethics and cost — ~2:20]**
Due care is doing the right thing — acting as a prudent person would. Due
diligence is verifying that it is being done. Failing at due care is
negligence, and the exam loves that word. Keep them straight like this: due
care is driving carefully; due diligence is checking the brakes were actually
serviced.

Then the ISC2 Code of Ethics, four canons applied in order when they conflict:
protect society, the common good, necessary public trust and confidence, and
the infrastructure; act honourably, honestly, justly, responsibly and legally;
provide diligent and competent service to principals; advance and protect the
profession. The order matters because exam scenarios make the canons fight each
other — and the earlier canon wins.

Finally, cost. A control should never cost more than the value of the asset it
protects. More precisely, its annual cost should be less than the reduction in
annualised loss expectancy it delivers. You don't hire a ten-thousand-dollar
guard for a one-thousand-dollar laptop. And defence in depth layers
administrative, technical and physical controls so no single failure is fatal.
One lock can be picked; a policy, a firewall and a locked door together are a
different story.

**[SECTION — The exam and the credential — ~3:55]**
The English CISSP exam uses computerised adaptive testing — CAT. You get
between 100 and 150 items in three hours, and the passing score is 700 out of
1000. Adaptive means the exam adjusts to you: answer well and the questions get
harder; struggle and they get easier. It's measuring your ability level, not
just your knowledge.

Full certification needs five years of paid work in two or more of the eight
domains. A relevant degree or an approved certification can waive one year.
Pass the exam without the experience and you become an Associate of ISC2 while
you earn it — a legitimate credential in its own right, not a consolation
prize.

**[COMMON MISTAKES — ~4:45]**
Three traps. One: choosing the answer the engineer would pick instead of the
one the risk advisor would pick. If the option involves a cool tool but skips
the business, it's probably a distractor. Two: confusing due care with due
diligence. Doing the right thing is care; verifying it is done is diligence.
Three: thinking accountability can be delegated. Responsibility moves down the
org chart; accountability stays with senior management, permanently.

**[RECAP — ~5:10]**
Human safety beats everything, every time. Think like a risk advisor to the
business, not a technician. Assess the risk and the business requirements
before choosing any control — and when in doubt, gather facts and consult
stakeholders first. Due care means doing the right thing; due diligence means
verifying it. The ethics canons apply in order: society, honour, principals,
profession. A control must cost less than the loss it prevents. Defence in
depth layers administrative, technical and physical controls. The exam is CAT:
100 to 150 items, three hours, 700 to pass. And the credential wants five years
across two domains — or Associate status while you earn them.
